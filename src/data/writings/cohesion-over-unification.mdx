---
title: Cohesion Over Unification
description:
  Unifying design systems across apps used to be a massive multiplier. Now I
  think the real multiplier is a cohesion layer that lets each app build for its
  platform.
date: 2026-09-30
featured: false
status: published
---

so i had a thought this morning about where our apps are heading around
[design systems](/work/breakline-platform) and centralized style repositories
and whatnot, and whether unifying everything is still the right move.

a coworker said something like, we have these two applications at work, and
there's stuff shared between them, really just utilities, but the styling is
different. the colors are similar, but component-tree-wise it's like you took a
combination of compact elements versus looser elements, paired that with
different layout patterns, and branded those as two separate intra-app brands.
and he kind of asked, why should they be separate?

his brain is focused on the past, where you had companies like
[seek](https://seek-oss.github.io/braid-design-system/) out of australia, or
other apps, where the separation wasn't worth it. centralizing style consistency
was a really big productivity increase, a massive multiplier for teams. and that
was true.

but look at building native, or building for multi-platform with separate
codebases and separate languages. having two or even three separate
implementations just isn't really a problem anymore. and the same thing is
probably going to be even more true on the design system front. should we be
optimizing for consistency in styling and patterns? that was true three years
ago, but it's less true now, just like with the native question.

the real multiplier is some type of cohesion layer. you want it to look like it
was designed by the same company and the same designers, but not a carbon copy
of every implementation detail, right? a good example is the
[github ios app](https://github.com/mobile) versus the
[web app](https://github.com), or vercel on ios versus
[vercel on the web](https://vercel.com). the controls, the smaller lower-level
primitives, are more adaptive to the platform. the buttons look like
[ios buttons](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/buttons),
and that's a good thing.

so we should follow that path of building for the platform. each app should look
distinctly like itself. there's still some shared layer, some shared
productivity, that's really worth doing. it just doesn't look like it used to,
where it's unification across the stacks.
